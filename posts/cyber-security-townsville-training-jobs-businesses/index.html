Cyber security in Townsville: training, jobs, businesses and what is missing

Posted Sep 14, 2026 Updated Sep 14, 2026
By Nicholas “Nick” O'Sullivan, Townsville 10 min read

My companion article asks what became of the Townsville Cyber Security Innovation Node, the specific initiative three levels of government announced in 2020. This one asks a different question: setting that history aside, what does cyber security capability in Townsville actually look like today, for a resident who wants training, a business that wants help, or an employer looking for staff?

The short version: local capability hasn’t disappeared. Training pathways exist, employers advertise real roles, commercial providers operate here, and a professional community still meets. What’s harder to find is anything doing the job the 2020 node was meant to do: connecting those pieces into a regional strategy.

It’s also worth naming the political backdrop, since it changes the “who’s responsible” question. Jenny Hill was mayor when the node was announced in 2020. She was succeeded in March 2024 by Troy Thompson, who resigned in September 2025 after a review found he had misrepresented his military and academic history. Nick Dametto won the resulting by-election and was sworn in as Townsville’s 48th mayor on 24 November 2025. Three mayors, one council, and (as far as I can establish) no public statement from any of them about the cyber node specifically. That leaves a fair, open question for the current Council: does it see a role for itself in regional cyber capability, or was that always meant to be AustCyber’s job?

Training pathways

Training was part of the original 2020 proposition, and Council specifically named TAFE Queensland’s Certificate IV in Cyber Security at Pimlico as the local pathway. Checking TAFE’s current course page, its advertised study regions are Greater Brisbane, Gold Coast, Sunshine Coast, and Darling Downs and South West, plus online study. Townsville (Pimlico) appears in the page’s campus-selection information, but I could not identify a dated, publicly advertised Pimlico classroom intake.

I want to be fair to what I actually know here, separate from what the website shows: through my own professional network, I’m aware of North Queensland IT professionals (people already working in the field, building specific cyber-analysis skills rather than starting from scratch) who have completed Certificate IV units through TAFE’s Pimlico campus. That’s first-hand knowledge, not something I can point you to on TAFE’s public timetable, so treat it as exactly that: a reason to ask TAFE directly about current intakes rather than assume the course has quietly disappeared, but not a substitute for a dated, bookable enrolment. If you’re considering it, the useful question is specific: is there a confirmed face-to-face Pimlico intake, when does it start, and what does it involve?

Further north, TAFE Queensland’s Cairns campus has a purpose-built cyber security training facility, completed in 2023, for introductory and advanced IT security and networking training, a concrete example of regional training infrastructure actually being built.

James Cook University’s Bachelor of Information Technology lists Townsville and Cairns among its locations, with cybersecurity among its subject areas alongside networking, cloud computing and software engineering. Don’t confuse that with JCU’s separately named Bachelor of Cybersecurity. JCU’s own course information identifies that degree as available at JCU Singapore only. A degree carrying the university’s name isn’t automatically a Townsville course.

Two newer, non-accredited options are also worth watching rather than treating as confirmed offers. TAFE’s Queensland Future Skills Partnership page lists a free cyber security microcredential, subject to expression of interest and selection. Queensland separately announced $11.5 million in July 2026 to expand a small-business microcredential program with UniSC, including new cyber security, AI and procurement courses, but Business Queensland’s current page says those courses are still being developed. I covered the earlier version of this program when it was first announced; the pattern is consistent: announcements arrive well ahead of a bookable course.

Jobs and the professional community

My review of cyber security job advertisements across North Queensland found real roles across security, identity, risk and infrastructure, a smaller market than Brisbane, Sydney or Melbourne, but a genuine one. Queensland Health’s Senior Director, Digital Health, Information and Technology role based in Mount Isa, which closed on 8 June 2026, is one example of security responsibility embedded in a broader regional technology role.

ACS’s Townsville Chapter keeps a professional community meeting locally: its account of an AI-versus-AI event on 1 October 2025 documents a cyber security session held at Smart Precinct NQ. I also shared a free AI and cyber skills workshop for Townsville small businesses that ran on 8 September 2026, a recent local opportunity, if not one you can still book.

What appears to be missing

This is where the coordination gap from my companion article actually bites.

Smart Precinct NQ (the broader innovation organisation that hosted some of this activity, though never the cyber node itself) has wound down. Its former chief executive Cassandra Cazzulino publicly said in 2026 that its operational grant funding wouldn’t be renewed, reporting that SPNQ had supported more than 650 businesses over its lifetime. SPNQ subsequently confirmed that “The Power of Her Story” on 24 June 2026 (marking the graduation of its second Female Founders cohort) was its final public event, and that “our public programs and events have now concluded,” while adding it was still exploring ways to support the ecosystem in future. That’s a wind-down of public delivery, not a confirmed legal closure, and its own website still describes current programs, a reminder that an organisation’s website can outlast its service delivery.

Queensland’s own small-business cyber programs have also had a rough few years. In June 2024, Queensland announced a $15 million procurement for a free small-business cyber security solution. The resulting Cryptoloc contract didn’t last: at estimates on 8 August 2025, Minister Steve Minnikin told Parliament the contract had been terminated on 8 January 2025, after an initial $1.938 million payment, with the department seeking recovery. ABC reporting in June 2026, drawing on an internal procurement audit, identified weaknesses in the financial checks around that contract.

Queensland then partnered with Cyber Wardens in August 2025, a national, Commonwealth-funded small-business training program delivered by COSBOA. That’s also over: Cyber Wardens’ Commonwealth funding ended 31 July 2026, with COSBOA delivery ceasing 11 September 2026, a date that has now passed. Its old web address redirects to a delivery partner’s case study rather than an active course.

None of that proves any individual program failed on its own terms. But taken together, it’s a genuine pattern: short-lived, differently-branded programs, each requiring a business owner or careers adviser to relearn what’s currently available. That’s exactly the connective, ongoing role the 2020 node announcement described, and exactly what seems to be missing now.

The commercial market still exists

Winding down a grant-funded coordination body doesn’t mean Townsville has lost its practitioners. NQIT lists managed IT and cyber protection services, with offices in Townsville and Mount Isa. Q10 Systems advertises cyber security services from Townsville and Cairns. These are examples of the market, not a ranking or a complete directory.

The Townsville Christian College ransomware incident I covered in August is a reminder of why that local capability matters: schools and other community institutions depend on people who understand both the technology and the consequences of losing access to it.

I should disclose my own position here: I founded Suburban Secure, and provide commercial cyber security and IT advice to Townsville small businesses, charities and community organisations through it, one part of that same commercial market, not a neutral outside observer of it. I’ve written more about why I still take on that consulting work.

Where to get help now

For a step-by-step guide to current, free assistance (IDCARE, the ACSC Small Business Hub, what to ask a commercial provider, and which older programs have quietly ended), I’ve written a practical companion piece on Suburban Secure: Where can Townsville small businesses get cyber security help?

The open question

My companion article ends with four questions the public record can’t currently answer about the original node. Reading the current landscape alongside that history adds a fifth: with three mayors having held office since the node was announced, and the organisations that hosted or adjoined it wound down or restructured, does Townsville City Council see a role for itself in coordinating regional cyber capability today, or has that always been someone else’s job?

I don’t think the answer is that Townsville has failed at cyber security. Local people, training providers and businesses that need their skills are all still here. The open question is who, if anyone, is meant to be connecting them.
