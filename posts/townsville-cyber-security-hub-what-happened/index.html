Townsville was going to be a cyber security hub. What happened?

Posted Sep 14, 2026 Updated Sep 14, 2026
By Nicholas “Nick” O'Sullivan, Townsville 8 min read

Updated 14 September 2026: this article has been split in two and tightened to a strict primary-source standard. The current landscape (training, jobs, commercial providers and what’s missing today) now has its own companion piece: Cyber security in Townsville: training, jobs, businesses and what is missing.

In June 2020, governments announced that Townsville would join Australia’s national network of cyber security innovation nodes. Six years later, I wanted to know what became of that specific, named initiative: not cyber security in North Queensland generally, but the funded program itself.

This is deliberately a narrow piece. I have only included claims I can trace to a primary source: a Council announcement, a government publication, an organisation’s own statement, an archived job listing. Where the trail runs out, I say so rather than guess at what filled the gap.

What was announced

The joint Commonwealth and Queensland announcement on 2 June 2020 proposed Cyber Security Innovation Nodes in Brisbane, Townsville and the Sunshine Coast, delivered by AustCyber in partnership with the Queensland Government and the two regional councils.

Townsville City Council’s announcement the following day, under then-Mayor Jenny Hill, said the node would put Townsville “at the forefront” of cyber capability development, give local businesses access comparable with the capital cities, and sit alongside Smart Precinct NQ and the North Queensland Regional Data Centre. Council specifically pointed to the Certificate IV in Cyber Security at TAFE Queensland’s Pimlico campus as a training pathway.

On 17 March 2021, Council announced the node had launched and its inaugural manager had been appointed. The stated next steps were training opportunities, forums, seminars, and cyber security health checks for local businesses.

That gives a clear set of commitments to measure against: people trained, businesses helped, relationships built, a healthier local security posture.

The role itself was real

It’s worth being specific about what “the node” actually was, because press releases can make an initiative sound more abstract than it was.

AustCyber’s own job listing for the role, archived by the Wayback Machine in November 2020, since removed from AustCyber’s current site, describes a genuine, funded position: a two-year AustCyber contract, 15 to 22.5 hours a week, $120,000–$145,000 pro rata. Its responsibilities included establishing an industry advisory group, delivering cyber security events across North Queensland, and (this line matters) “maintain[ing] node activities and deliverables within budgetary and resource constraints and meet[ing] evaluation and reporting requirements.”

AustCyber itself expected reporting against outcomes. I have not found that reporting published anywhere.

Smart Precinct NQ and the cyber node were different things

The organisations and projects in this story are easily collapsed into a single “cyber hub.” That makes the funding story harder to understand, and it’s worth being precise here because the two are frequently confused.

Smart Precinct NQ was a broader innovation and entrepreneurship organisation. Its work covered business development, founders, mentoring and connections between industry, government and research. The cyber node was a specific, separately funded initiative operating within that wider environment.

AustCyber’s August 2021 submission to Home Affairs explains the national funding model: most of AustCyber’s funding came from Commonwealth grants, with contractual arrangements with state governments and with Townsville and Sunshine Coast councils, matched by AustCyber to deliver the node network. That establishes participation by all three levels of government, without supplying a separate Townsville total.

The 2019 Townsville City Deal progress report records SPNQ’s incorporation and a $1.5 million Townsville City Council commitment to support business growth and investment. Queensland’s Regional Enablers recipients list separately records $298,986 for SPNQ’s NQ Regional Enablers Program, focused on sustainability and circular-economy innovation.

Neither figure is a stand-alone budget for the Townsville cyber node.

Adding these figures together would not produce a reliable total for “money spent on Townsville cyber security.” I have not established a separate, reconciled Townsville node budget from the sources reviewed for this article. That is a gap worth addressing, particularly if governments want the community to assess what the investment achieved.

Then the trail goes cold

Townsville was still being counted as an active node as late as 2022: Stone & Chalk’s Australia’s Cyber Security Sector Competitiveness Plan 2022 states that “three Cyber Security Innovation nodes have been established in Queensland since June 2020; in Brisbane, Townsville and the Sunshine Coast.”

After that, the Department of Industry’s account of the Industry Growth Centres Initiative sets out the relevant dates: AustCyber merged with Stone & Chalk in February 2021, AustCyber’s contract with the Australian Government ended in June 2024, and the wider Industry Growth Centres program ended on 31 December 2024. The department lists a national network of innovation nodes among AustCyber’s former activities.

None of that establishes a Townsville-specific closure date, or tells us whether the node’s local functions (the advisory group, the events, the health checks) continued under a different arrangement, were formally wound up, or simply stopped without an announcement. I have not found a close-out evaluation, a handover notice, or any public reconciliation of what the node delivered against its 2021 commitments.

One thing is worth separating out clearly, because it’s easy to conflate. Smart Precinct NQ went through its own, later wind-down: its former chief executive publicly said in 2026 that its operational grant funding would not be renewed, and SPNQ itself subsequently confirmed that its public programs and events had concluded after a final event on 24 June 2026. That is a 2026 event affecting a different, broader organisation. It should not be read as evidence of when the Townsville cyber node itself stopped operating. By the evidence above, that had already faded from public view years earlier. The companion article to this one covers what SPNQ’s closure means for the current landscape.

Four questions the public record doesn’t answer

  1. What happened to the Townsville node’s functions after AustCyber’s Commonwealth contract ended in 2024?
  2. What funding was allocated and spent specifically on the node, and what outcomes were recorded against the “evaluation and reporting requirements” AustCyber itself specified?
  3. Was the node ever formally closed or handed over, and if so, to whom?
  4. Did the training, forums and business health checks promised in March 2021 actually happen, and at what scale?

Where this leaves things

I’m not concluding that the Townsville Cyber Security Innovation Node failed. I don’t have the evidence to say that, and an absent report is not proof that the work didn’t happen; it may have occurred and simply left a poor public trail.

What I can say is that a publicly funded regional initiative, backed by three levels of government and specific enough to have its own job description and budget, does not have a public record six years later that lets a resident answer basic questions about what it delivered. That is the part of this story that remains unresolved, and it’s a fair thing to ask Council, the Queensland Government and whoever now holds AustCyber’s records to fix.
