A classroom app can begin with a very reasonable request: a teacher has found something that could help students learn. It works in a browser, the demonstration looks useful, and getting a class started appears straightforward.

Before students sign in, though, someone needs to understand what they are being invited into. Who can contact them? What happens to their work and conversations? Which features can the school control? If a student encounters something harmful, who will respond?

Those questions are a useful way into Australia's proposed digital duty of care.

A friend's recent LinkedIn post prompted me to look more closely at the proposal and what schools might take from it. I support the principle that providers should take responsibility for foreseeable harm through their services. I also think the legislation deserves careful scrutiny. Schools can strengthen their own decisions about technology while that debate continues.

The government says it introduced the **Online Safety Amendment (Digital Duty of Care) Bill 2026** on **15 October 2026**. The department's [20 October announcement](https://www.infrastructure.gov.au/department/media/news/digital-duty-care-bill-introduced) describes proposed requirements covering services including games, social media and AI chatbots, with penalties of up to $109.2 million for non-compliance. These are proposed obligations, rather than a standard schools should describe as already in force.

The [government's overview](https://www.infrastructure.gov.au/media-communications/internet/online-safety/digital-duty-care) describes a duty for online providers to take reasonable steps to keep Australians safe, including additional protection for under-18s. Its reach extends beyond removing individual posts: harmful conduct and service features also matter. The accompanying reforms include giving social media users more choice over algorithmically recommended feeds.

That attention to design is valuable. A service's defaults, contact settings and recommendations can shape what a young person encounters. A complaints button is useful, but I would also want a provider to explain what it has done to reduce the chance of harm occurring.

There are legitimate questions about how far the law should go. In its [submission on the September exposure draft](https://humanrights.gov.au/our-work/submissions/technology-and-human-rights/digital-duty-of-care-exposure-bill), the Australian Human Rights Commission supported a digital duty of care while seeking clearer definitions of harm, narrower ministerial rule-making powers and stronger consideration of human rights. Freedom of expression and privacy belong in that discussion alongside protection from harm.

That submission concerned an earlier version. The [Minister's announcement of the introduced bill](https://minister.infrastructure.gov.au/wells/media-release/digital-duty-care-next-step-australias-world-leading-reforms) says it clarifies exemptions for professional news and entertainment media and provides parliamentary scrutiny of additional harms. Those changes deserve examination on their merits. My concern is that uncertain boundaries, combined with substantial penalties, could encourage providers to restrict legitimate information too broadly. Clear thresholds, proportionate enforcement and meaningful ways to challenge decisions matter.

For schools, this is a prompt to review practice. It does not establish that every school buying software will acquire the same duties or penalties as a platform provider. Schools already have responsibilities towards their students; eSafety's [guidance on incidents outside school hours](https://www.esafety.gov.au/sites/default/files/2020-02/prepare_5_-_tips_for_responding_to_incidents_that_happen_outside_school_hours.pdf) explicitly connects schools' duty of care with responding to foreseeable harm. The proposed platform regime and a school's existing responsibilities need to be understood separately.

My perspective comes from [work across Queensland state-school environments and independent-school IT](/about/), including infrastructure, cloud platforms, device management and vendor coordination. In my state-school work, reviewing applications often involved consulting a risk review register. I value that approach because it gives staff a shared starting point for assessing a tool and understanding the risks attached to its use.

For independent schools, one useful resource is [Safer Technologies 4 Schools (ST4S)](https://st4s.edu.au/). Its reach also includes government and Catholic schools across Australia, as well as New Zealand. Administered by Education Services Australia, it provides a consistent approach to assessing digital products, including their security and privacy controls. Its [assessment process](https://st4s.edu.au/general-information/) examines supplier responses and supporting information, producing information about risks and suggested treatments that schools can use in their decisions.

Authorised school and education-sector staff can use the [ST4S Catalogue](https://st4s.edu.au/st4s-catalogue/) to find products, check assessment status and access completed reports. That can reduce duplicated work and give a school a stronger basis for questioning a supplier.

I would use the report as evidence in the school's review, then check how its findings apply to the proposed activity, student age group and configuration. A product's presence in a catalogue or display of a badge should prompt staff to read the assessment and its conditions. The school still needs to decide whether the particular use is appropriate and record how it will address the risks.

A school needs technology that supports teaching and can be managed with the people and resources available. The educational purpose, technical configuration and arrangements for student support all need to fit together. A successful sign-in tells us very little about that wider picture.

Consider a hypothetical school reviewing an app for writing practice. A teacher sees a useful way to give students more feedback. IT checks the account permissions and how student access will be removed. A wellbeing leader asks what happens if a student discloses distress to the app's conversational assistant.

Each person has identified something the others might miss. The teacher understands the lesson and the students. IT understands the system's access and limitations. The wellbeing team understands the school's response arrangements. Leadership needs to settle the conditions of use and resource any work those conditions create.

Now imagine the supplier adds an AI assistant to an already approved product. The school should revisit its decision: the new feature may collect different information or introduce interactions the original review never considered. Approval needs a scope and a review trigger, including significant changes to features, terms or data handling.

I would want a short, usable record for each significant student-facing service:

| Decision | What the school should be able to explain |
| --- | --- |
| Educational purpose | Which students will use it, for what activity, and why it is suitable for their age and needs. |
| Information handling | What data is collected, who receives it, how long it is retained, and whether student inputs can be used for AI training. |
| Safety and settings | Which interactions are possible, which features are enabled, and what safeguards have been checked. |
| Human response | How a concern reaches an appropriate staff member, who takes over when they are unavailable, and how families receive support. |
| Ownership and review | Who owns the decision, when it will be revisited, and how the school can suspend or leave the service. |

The depth of review should match the use. A teacher preparing a worksheet from public information presents different questions from a chatbot having private conversations with students. For AI, schools already have a national reference: the [Australian Framework for Generative Artificial Intelligence in Schools](https://www.education.gov.au/schooling/resources/australian-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence-ai-schools) supports responsible and ethical use across the school community. It gives schools a starting point for their own decisions.

Another area I have looked at is the introduction of filtering and monitoring tools that give schools greater visibility of student activity. What interests me is whether relevant information can reach the people responsible for wellbeing early enough to help. In a workflow where logs are reviewed only after someone requests an investigation, that opportunity may be missed.

It helps to distinguish what different tools can actually see. [DNS filtering](https://developers.cloudflare.com/learning-paths/cybersafe/concepts/what-is-dns-filtering/) makes decisions about domain names. It can prevent access to a blocked website, but DNS alone does not reveal the words a student types into a Google search or the content of the resulting page. Allowing access to Google therefore says very little about the nature of a particular search. A DNS log cannot supply search terms it never captured.

Some filtering and monitoring products offer additional visibility into searches and other activity, depending on the browser, device software, integrations and inspection settings in use. For example, [Linewize's documentation](https://help.linewize.com/hc/en-gb/articles/5999388552732-Linewize-Filter-Red-Flag-Alert-Email) describes alerts for wellbeing and school leadership staff that can include search terms where those terms can be captured. Its [reporting controls](https://help.linewize.com/hc/en-gb/articles/5803919155100-Set-up-Filtering-Reports-and-Alerts) also allow reports and alerts to be scoped to particular students or groups. Schools need to verify the coverage of their own deployment.

Imagine a student searching for help with bullying on an allowed search engine. With suitable monitoring configured, that activity might prompt an alert even though the student has not visited a blocked website. A designated wellbeing staff member could then consider the context and whether support is needed. This is a hypothetical example of how information can lead to a response while the student still has access to a legitimate resource.

**The useful change is connecting relevant information with someone who can act on it.** Where more detailed activity is captured, leaving it in an IT log until an investigation is requested creates a different process from routing a concern to an authorised wellbeing contact. IT helps establish reliable collection, permissions and delivery; wellbeing staff bring the context and professional judgement needed to decide what happens next.

The same need for context applies if a monitoring system flags a student's writing as concerning. The text might be an assignment, a quotation, a disclosure or something else entirely. A flag needs context and an appropriate human response. Treating it as proof of misconduct risks making a poor decision about the student.

The school should establish who receives alerts, how urgency is assessed, what happens during absences and which arrangements apply outside staffed hours. Those responsibilities need realistic resourcing. Families should understand the service's coverage and limitations, including whether activity away from the school network is covered. A quiet dashboard should never become a reason to dismiss a concern raised directly by a student.

Privacy belongs inside the same decision. I would ask what monitoring actually captures, which staff can see it, whether access is recorded, and when information is deleted. Access should be limited to the students and information a staff member needs for their role. A relevant alert with enough context may be sufficient; access to a student's wider search history needs a clear purpose and appropriate authorisation. Students and families need an understandable explanation of those arrangements. Collecting sensitive information creates responsibilities of its own, even when the original purpose is protective.

I would also want a way to review an inappropriate block. Imagine a student trying to access a legitimate health resource or a teacher preparing a lesson about discrimination. The school needs an efficient process for assessing the educational context while maintaining appropriate protections. The balance between safety and access is something schools have to practise in their own systems.

Students should help shape that work. They can explain where reporting is confusing, which tools they actually use and whether the school's guidance makes sense. eSafety's [Best Practice Framework for Online Safety Education](https://www.esafety.gov.au/educators/best-practice-framework) supports student participation and an approach involving the whole school community.

Teaching can then connect directly to the tools in front of students: why an app requests information, how a recommendation differs from a deliberate choice, how to question an AI response, and where to seek help. Students need opportunities to exercise judgement, alongside adults and providers taking responsibility for the environments they use.

This connects with my earlier argument about [school cybersecurity governance]({% post_url 2026-10-04-beyond-essential-eight-school-cybersecurity-governance %}): technology decisions should trace back to learning, wellbeing and the school's purpose. Leadership needs evidence that arrangements work, such as completed reviews, practised escalation processes and feedback from students and teachers. The number of blocked pages alone says little about those outcomes.

A practical first step would be to bring a teaching leader, IT, student wellbeing and a member of the executive together around one widely used platform. Walk through its settings, its information handling and a plausible student concern. Record the gaps, assign the follow-up work and agree when to check it again.

The parliamentary debate should produce clear, proportionate obligations for providers. In the meantime, a school can make one concrete improvement: ensure that every important digital tool has an educational purpose, understood safeguards and someone accountable for what happens after students begin using it.
