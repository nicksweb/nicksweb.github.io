# Story plan: UTS's $20m-plus network modernisation — what happens before the rollout?

> **Working document for nickosullivan.id.au**  
> Prepared: 17 September 2026  
> Status: research/outline, not yet a finished article

## Recommended approach

I think this works best as a **two-part series**, because there are really two stories here:

1. **Part 1 — the procurement and planning story:** the public announcement is about a $20m-plus build, but the public record shows UTS began defining the future network in 2023. This gives a strong angle on requirements gathering, vendor-neutral strategy, procurement governance, proof-of-concept testing and the amount of work that happens before equipment is rolled out.
2. **Part 2 — how campus networking has changed:** compare the UTS network documented in a 2015 Alcatel-Lucent Enterprise case study with the 2026 Extreme/Nexon announcement. The change is not simply faster Wi-Fi. Modern platforms increasingly combine wired, wireless, security, visibility, automation and AI-assisted operations. This part can ask what that means operationally, while being careful not to claim technical details UTS has not published.

This split also gives room for a personal perspective without turning the first article into a product comparison.

# Possible story angles

## Angle A — Recommended: "The rollout is the last visible step"

**Thesis:** When a major technology rollout finally becomes public, much of the important work may already have happened. The UTS announcement provides a public example of the process: define the desired future state, seek vendor-neutral advice, establish a procurement process, test proposed solutions, select a supplier, then move into an 18-month build.

This is the strongest fit with my own background. During my time working in statewide systems support for Queensland state education, I was involved in projects that deployed equipment across large numbers of schools. At that scale, the physical rollout is only one part of the job. Requirements, testing, standardisation, supportability, logistics and the consequences of getting a design wrong all matter before equipment reaches a site.

**Possible headline:**

> **Before the rollout: what UTS's $20m network upgrade tells us about enterprise technology procurement**

Alternative:

> **A $20m network refresh starts long before the first access point is installed**

---

## Angle B — "Wireless by default, wired by exception"

UTS used this phrase publicly in its 2023 procurement material. It is a strong hook because it shows the desired outcome was being described years before a technology vendor was announced.

The useful nuance is that UTS did **not** say Ethernet was disappearing. Its 2023 tender explicitly anticipated wired connections remaining for high-bandwidth uses and systems such as IoT, security and audiovisual equipment.

**Possible headline:**

> **'Wireless by default, wired by exception': tracing UTS's network strategy from 2023 to a $20m Wi-Fi 7 build**

This angle could work particularly well for a technical audience because it moves beyond the usual "new Wi-Fi is faster" story.

---

## Angle C — "Networks are becoming operating platforms"

Compare the language around the 2015 UTS network with the 2026 project.

The 2015 case study concentrated on switching, wireless access points, network management and a resilient Shortest Path Bridging architecture. In 2026, the announcement talks about an integrated platform spanning wired and wireless networking, security, AI capabilities and automation.

The important argument is **not** that automation suddenly appeared in 2026. Network automation has existed for years. The change is that automation, telemetry, policy, security and AI-assisted troubleshooting are increasingly being packaged into the core management platform instead of being separate specialist tools or scripts.

**Possible headline:**

> **From switches and access points to automated platforms: what has changed in campus networking?**

This is better as Part 2 than as the announcement story itself.

---

## Angle D — "The technology lifecycle repeats, even when the technology changes"

There is a nice historical symmetry here. An Alcatel-Lucent Enterprise case study says a major UTS network implementation took place in January 2015 and supported more than 37,000 users. By 2023, UTS told iTnews its network was close to end-of-life and began planning the next strategic direction.

That gives a useful infrastructure lifecycle angle: today's "future-ready" design eventually becomes tomorrow's legacy environment. Good architecture therefore needs to consider not only capability on day one, but supportability, migration, skills and the next replacement cycle.

**Before the rollout: inside the planning behind UTS's $20m-plus network modernisation**

## Suggested standfirst / excerpt

The University of Technology Sydney has announced a $20 million-plus network modernisation with Nexon Asia Pacific and Extreme Networks. But the public trail shows the project began years earlier, with UTS first defining a vendor-neutral future-state network and then bringing in procurement expertise before selecting a solution. For anyone who has worked on large technology rollouts, that less-visible planning may be the most interesting part of the story.

## Main argument

Do not write this primarily as "UTS bought Wi-Fi 7". The more distinctive story is that we can see several stages of a large technology procurement in public:

**strategic requirements -> vendor-neutral advice -> procurement consultancy -> market engagement/open tender -> design and proof of concept -> 18-month build -> ongoing managed services/support**

There is enough evidence to describe those stages, although we should avoid claiming that every document found is part of one uninterrupted formal procurement unless UTS confirms it.

## Suggested opening

A major technology rollout can look deceptively simple once it reaches the announcement stage. A supplier is named, a dollar figure appears, the new platform is described and eventually equipment starts turning up on site.

The difficult work often starts much earlier.

That is what makes the University of Technology Sydney's newly announced $20 million-plus network modernisation interesting. UTS has selected Nexon Asia Pacific to deliver a new wired and wireless environment based around Extreme Networks technology, including Wi-Fi 7 and Extreme Platform ONE. The design and proof-of-concept stages are complete and the project is now moving into an 18-month build.

But the public trail begins years before that announcement.

In February 2023, UTS sought professional services to develop a **technology vendor-agnostic Network Strategic Direction**. Its desired future state was described as **"Wireless by default, Wired by exception"**. Later that month, the university separately sought a procurement consultancy to help it engage the enterprise networking market.

That sequence is familiar to anyone who has been involved in technology deployment at scale. During my time as a State-Wide Systems Technician in Queensland state education, I was involved in projects that rolled equipment out across hundreds of high schools. The rollout is the visible part. Before it, teams have to decide what problem they are actually trying to solve, what a supportable standard looks like, how proposed solutions will be tested and what happens when a design that works in a lab meets a real production environment.

> **Personal accuracy note:** adjust the wording above so it describes only projects I was personally involved in and does not imply I was responsible for procurement decisions unless that is accurate.

## Section 3 — The public trail starts in 2023

This is the central section of Part 1.

On **2 February 2023**, UTS published tender **UTS-1046910**, seeking professional services to develop a new networking strategy.

The tender described:

- a multi-site campus across **Ultimo, Moore Park and Botany**;
- increasing use of cloud partners;
- a desire for a more secure and flexible network supporting hybrid teaching;
- a future state of **"Wireless by default, Wired by exception"**;
- most suitable endpoints moving to Wi-Fi;
- continued wired connectivity for requirements including high-bandwidth use, IoT, security and AV;
- maintaining internal service levels; and
- training UTS network staff.

Crucially, UTS asked for a **technology vendor-agnostic Network Strategic Direction**. That is worth emphasising. At this point in the visible process, the university was publicly describing outcomes and architecture principles rather than naming the product it wanted to buy.

## Section 4 — UTS then separated procurement advice from the technology tender

On **27 February 2023**, UTS sought a procurement consultancy to assist with industry engagement resulting from the strategy work.

One particularly interesting detail is the probity condition: the successful procurement consultant would be **prohibited from participating in the later enterprise network tenders** resulting from the program.

That is a useful example of procurement governance. The adviser helping structure the market engagement could not then turn around and compete to supply the resulting network.

## Section 6 — Why this process resonates with large-scale education IT

This is where the article becomes personal rather than simply repeating trade press.

### Suggested discussion points

- Large deployments reward standardisation because every exception becomes a support burden.
- A design that works for one building or one test environment may behave very differently across hundreds of sites or tens of thousands of users.
- Proof-of-concept testing is valuable because specifications do not tell you everything about interoperability, management workflows, failure modes and the day-to-day experience of support staff.
- Requirements gathering should include operational questions: Who will manage it? How is it monitored? What skills are needed? How is a failed component replaced? What is the migration path? What is the support model after go-live?
- The people operating the platform after the project team leaves should matter in the design.

### Possible first-person passage

> One lesson I took from working on statewide education technology projects is that scale changes the question. A product can be technically capable and still be the wrong operational fit. When the same design has to work across large numbers of sites, you start thinking about repeatability, remote visibility, failure recovery, configuration consistency and what the support team will face on a Monday morning after the project has moved on.

> That is why the UTS procurement trail is more interesting to me than the headline dollar figure. The documents show the university first defining a future state, then seeking independent procurement expertise, and the eventual announcement says design and proof-of-concept work happened before the 18-month build began.

Keep this framed as **my experience and interpretation**. Do not imply that UTS followed the same internal process as Queensland state education beyond what is visible in the public documents.

## Section 7 — The proof of concept matters

The 2026 announcement confirms a design and proof-of-concept phase occurred before the build.

This creates a good opportunity to explain, at a high level, what a PoC can answer in a network project without pretending to know UTS's actual test plan.

### Phrase carefully

Instead of:

> UTS tested roaming, failover, NAC and Wi-Fi density during the proof of concept.

Use:

> The announcement does not disclose what UTS tested during its proof of concept. In projects of this type, however, a PoC can be used to validate areas such as interoperability, management workflows, resilience, policy enforcement, wireless behaviour and operational support before a design is committed to production.

That preserves the insight without inventing facts.

## Section 8 — The build is the next stage, not the beginning

Close Part 1 by returning to the main idea.

UTS is now entering an 18-month build, but the visible public history runs back to 2023. The most useful lesson is not which logo eventually won. It is that major infrastructure renewal is a process of defining the outcome, separating requirements from products, engaging the market, validating the design and only then scaling the deployment.

# Part 2

## Working title

**From switches and access points to automated platforms: how the campus network has changed**

### Alternatives

- What changed between UTS's 2015 network and its 2026 Wi-Fi 7 rebuild?
- A decade of campus networking: from SPB and AirManager to Wi-Fi 7 and AI-assisted operations
- UTS is replacing a decade-old campus network. The bigger change is how networks are operated.

## Main argument

The interesting technological shift is broader than Wi-Fi 7.

UTS's previous documented generation already contained sophisticated technology: high-density wireless, a resilient Shortest Path Bridging architecture and central network-management tooling. The 2026 generation adds another layer: integrated wired/wireless/security operations, richer telemetry, zero-touch configuration, policy automation and AI-assisted troubleshooting.

Be careful not to describe this as a clean jump from "manual" to "automated". The 2015 environment was not primitive, and automation predates Platform ONE. The story is about **consolidation and operational maturity**, not the invention of network automation.

## Section 4 — Network fabrics are not new either

This is a technically interesting nuance.

The ALE material says the previous UTS network used **Shortest Path Bridging**. Extreme's own material says **Extreme Fabric is built on SPB**.

That creates a potential continuity angle: the vendor and operating platform may be changing, while some underlying networking concepts are not necessarily new.

However, there is an important limitation: the sources reviewed do **not** conclusively establish that UTS is deploying Extreme Fabric as part of the 2026 solution. Platform ONE is confirmed; Extreme Fabric should only be discussed as part of the vendor's broader architecture unless a UTS-specific source confirms it.

## Section 5 — What "AI-ready" should and should not mean in the article

The announcement repeatedly connects the project with AI and an "AI-native" generation of students.

There are two distinct AI ideas here and they should not be blurred together:

1. **Demand on the network:** future students, researchers and applications may generate more bandwidth, devices and data-intensive workloads.
2. **AI in network operations:** the selected management platform itself includes AI-assisted/agentic operational capabilities.

The first is a strategic justification. The second is a product capability.

Avoid implying that Wi-Fi 7 is required simply because students use generative AI. Most AI inference may happen in cloud or data-centre infrastructure. The stronger network argument is the combined growth of endpoint density, real-time collaboration, multimedia, research workloads, smart-campus systems and new wireless capabilities.

---

## Section 6 — The operational change may matter more than raw speed

Potential personal reflection:

> It is easy to reduce a wireless upgrade to throughput numbers. In practice, the larger operational gain can come from consistency: knowing what is connected, having common policy, seeing faults centrally, deploying changes predictably and giving the support team enough context to diagnose a problem without physically standing beside every device.

This links nicely back to statewide support experience. When a team supports large numbers of sites or endpoints, the question becomes less "how fast is this switch?" and more "how consistently can we operate the whole environment?"

---

## Section 7 — A decade from now, this will be the legacy network

Finish on lifecycle rather than hype.

The 2015 network was itself presented as a modern, scalable platform supporting the future of education. In 2023, UTS described its then-current network as approaching end-of-life. The 2026 system will eventually go through the same lifecycle.

That gives the story a grounded ending:

> The most successful modernisation may therefore be the one that makes the next modernisation easier — through good standards, documentation, automation, staff capability and an architecture that can change without requiring the entire institution to change at once.

# Important accuracy / editorial notes

## Safe to state

- UTS publicly sought a vendor-neutral networking strategy in 2023.
- "Wireless by default, Wired by exception" came from UTS procurement material.
- UTS separately sought procurement consultancy support.
- The procurement consultancy RFT included a probity restriction preventing the successful consultant from participating in resulting enterprise network tenders.
- UTS told iTnews in 2023 that the network was close to end-of-life and that cybersecurity, monitoring and automation were expected improvements.
- Extreme named UTS as a customer in August 2026 and called it its largest Australian win.
- The September 2026 announcement identifies Nexon, Extreme Platform ONE, Wi-Fi 7, an open competitive tender, completed design/PoC work and an 18-month build.

## Phrase as interpretation

- "This appears to show a multi-year progression from strategy through procurement to delivery."
- "The public record gives us a partial view of how the project developed."
- "The chronology is consistent with a deliberate requirements-first procurement process."

Do not imply we have UTS's complete internal project record.

## Do not state without further evidence

- That a particular 2023 consultant designed the final Extreme solution.
- Which consultancy won either 2023 engagement unless an award notice is found.
- That every stage of the 2023 work flowed directly into the 2026 contract with no reset or change of scope.
- Exact AP or switch models.
- That Extreme Fabric is definitely part of the UTS build.
- That UTS is replacing ClearPass or any other named NAC platform.
- Authentication/EAP/certificate design.
- Exact number of APs, switches or network ports.
- A specific breakdown of the $20m-plus value between hardware, licensing, professional services and managed services.
- That "AI workloads" by themselves require Wi-Fi 7.
- That automation was absent from the previous UTS network.

# Questions worth putting to UTS / Nexon / Extreme before Part 2

If we want to turn Part 2 into original reporting rather than commentary, these are the questions I would send:

1. How many wired switches and wireless access points are included in the modernisation?
2. Which Extreme switch and Wi-Fi 7 AP models were selected?
3. Is Extreme Fabric/Fabric Connect part of the UTS production architecture?
4. What were the main technical scenarios tested during the proof of concept?
5. What factors carried the greatest weight in the tender: technical capability, operations, security, support model, cost, migration risk or something else?
6. How does "Wireless by default, Wired by exception" translate into the final 2026 design?
7. Which endpoint categories will remain wired?
8. What network-access-control and identity architecture is being used in the future state?
9. Will certificate-based authentication form part of the wireless access design?
10. What parts of Platform ONE's AI/automation capabilities does UTS expect to use in production?
11. What telemetry or operational data will be used to validate improvements after migration?
12. How will UTS train and transition its internal networking team onto the new platform?
13. Is the build being staged campus-by-campus, building-by-building or by network service?
14. How is coexistence with the incumbent ALE environment being handled during the 18-month transition?
15. What percentage of the $20m-plus engagement represents infrastructure versus software/subscriptions, professional services and ongoing managed services?

Even answers to four or five of these would substantially strengthen Part 2.

# Suggested source hierarchy

When writing the final article, prefer sources in roughly this order:

1. **UTS / original procurement documents** — best evidence of requirements and strategy.
2. **Extreme investor release** — primary vendor confirmation of the win.

4. **iTnews reporting** — useful independent secondary coverage, especially its April 2023 article.
5. **ALE historical case study/customer page** — useful for the previous network generation, but clearly vendor-produced marketing material.
6. **Extreme product pages** — useful for explaining what the platform can do in general, but not proof that UTS has enabled every capability.

For **Part 1**, resist the temptation to make Extreme Networks the centre of the article. The stronger original angle is the **three-year visible procurement trail** and what it says about large-scale infrastructure planning. Use the Nexon/Extreme announcement as the reason to revisit that process.

For **Part 2**, then use the technology selection as the jumping-off point to explore how enterprise campus networking has evolved: Wi-Fi 7, richer policy and security integration, centralised telemetry, zero-touch provisioning and AI-assisted operations. Keep a clear line between **capabilities Extreme sells generally** and **features UTS has publicly confirmed it will use**.

That gives the series a natural progression:

> **Part 1: How do you choose and validate a network at this scale?**  
> **Part 2: What has actually changed in the technology once you choose it?**
